Coward or Traitor?

A look at the question raised by Vicente Blanco-Capote’s book, its stated purpose, and the importance of examining the Bay of Pigs through documents and firsthand accounts.

Cover image supplied for this article. Volume 1, Coward or Traitor?

A question, not a verdict

The title Coward or Traitor? is deliberately confrontational. It asks readers to consider how responsibility should be understood when a covert operation ends in defeat and when decisions made far from the battlefield shape the fate of the people sent to fight. The question is a starting point for investigation, not a conclusion that can be settled by a cover or a slogan.

The book is by Vicente Blanco-Capote, identified on the cover as a member of Brigade 2506. The material provided for this article does not establish every argument developed in the full book, so readers should consult the publication itself before drawing conclusions about particular individuals or events. What can be examined directly is the author’s own explanation of why he wrote it.

Why the author says he wrote it

In the supplied prologue, Blanco-Capote explains that he considered asking a friend to write the prologue to his two books. When the friend changed his mind, the author says that writing it could have placed the friend in an uncomfortable position if controversy arose among admirers of President John F. Kennedy. This detail introduces one of the book’s central challenges: accounts of the Bay of Pigs remain emotionally charged, and interpretations of the operation can provoke strong disagreements.

Blanco-Capote then makes a modest claim about himself: he says he is not a writer, but that he likes the story and wants it to reflect what he considers the truth. That statement describes his purpose. It does not replace independent verification, and it does not mean every recollection or interpretation should automatically be accepted.

“A TOWN THAT HAVE NO MEMORY, SHOULD NOT EXIST”

The prologue closes with this declaration about memory. Its grammar is reproduced as it appears in the supplied text. The sentiment is clear: historical events should not disappear simply because time passes or because those who experienced them grow older. Preserving memory requires more than retelling a familiar story. It requires recording names, comparing accounts, keeping documents, and distinguishing what a source directly states from what later readers infer.

Firsthand experience and the documentary record

The author says that he participated from the beginning in “Operation Pluto” and that he found documents unknown to some Brigade members. That is a claim made in the prologue and a useful lead for further research. To evaluate the significance of those documents, readers would need to know what each document is, who created it, when it was produced, where it is preserved, and whether other records corroborate its contents.

Firsthand testimony can preserve details that official summaries leave out: what people were told, what they expected, what they saw, and how they understood events at the time. But memory and documents do different kinds of work. A recollection can explain a person’s experience; a dated record may help establish when an event occurred; several independent sources may support or challenge an interpretation. The strongest historical account makes those distinctions visible.

This approach matters for the Brigade 2506 story. The failed landing at the Bay of Pigs is often summarized through a few major decisions and a short chronology. The experience of the people involved, however, includes preparation, uncertainty, organization, communication, and decisions whose consequences may have been understood differently by different participants. A book based on personal writings and collected documents can provide leads for examining those dimensions, provided that its claims are read critically and compared with other evidence.

Reading the title in historical context

The words “coward” and “traitor” are moral judgments. They are not interchangeable, and neither should be applied casually. Calling someone a coward suggests a failure of courage; calling someone a traitor suggests a breach of loyalty. To decide whether either label is justified in a historical case, a reader needs more than the outcome of an operation. The relevant questions include what a person knew at the time, what authority the person had, what options were available, what orders were given, and what records survive.

That is why a question mark matters. It leaves the issue open for examination. Readers can approach the book asking what evidence supports the author’s position, what counterevidence exists, and whether the available records allow a confident conclusion. This is not a way to diminish the testimony of Brigade veterans. It is a way to take their testimony seriously enough to examine it carefully.

It is equally important not to confuse the historical subject with the author alone. Brigade 2506 was made up of many people whose experiences differed according to their roles, assignments, and circumstances. One participant’s account can illuminate a part of the story, but no single testimony should be treated as a substitute for the whole record. The broader history becomes richer when individual accounts are placed alongside operational records, contemporary reporting, photographs, correspondence, and other participants’ recollections.

What the prologue leaves readers wanting to know

The supplied prologue offers several questions that a careful reader can carry into the book. Which documents did the author discover, and where are they held today? Were they created during the events or collected afterward? Which parts of the narrative draw on the author’s own experience, and which are based on other people’s diaries and biographies? When accounts disagree, how does the author explain the differences?

These questions are not accusations. They are basic tools for reading history. They also help preserve a source’s value for future researchers. A document with a clear date, creator, provenance, and context is easier to assess than an image or quotation detached from its origins. A testimony that identifies what the witness saw personally, what someone else told him, and what he learned later gives readers a clearer basis for evaluation.

The prologue also invokes an Arab proverb about writing a book, planting a tree, and having a son. Blanco-Capote presents his work as a contribution made in honor of people who wrote diaries and biographies. In that sense, the book is framed as part of a chain of remembrance: one generation records its experience so another generation can study it.

Preserving such material is valuable even when readers ultimately disagree with an author’s conclusions. A record can remain useful as evidence of what a person believed, remembered, or wanted future generations to understand. The task of historical writing is to preserve that record while being clear about what it does—and does not—prove.

How to approach the book

Readers interested in Brigade 2506 can approach Coward or Traitor? with three questions in mind. First, what does the author report from personal experience? Second, what documents or earlier accounts does he use to support the narrative? Third, how do those claims compare with other available sources? Keeping those questions in view allows readers to engage with a passionate personal account without confusing interpretation with independently established fact.

The cover and prologue introduce a book concerned with memory, responsibility, and the lasting controversy surrounding the Bay of Pigs. The full work is needed to assess the author’s detailed arguments. This article therefore presents the author’s stated purpose and the questions it raises; it does not claim to summarize or verify the entire book.
